\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:2}
\omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:td}{Q-learning}: $-10 + 0.1(-3 - 5 + 10) = -9.8$. SARSA: $-10 + 0.1(-3 - 8 + 10) = -10.1$: it values the action by what the

\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:3}
Holding costs $0.2q^2$ per period after each sale: with one lot a period, $0.2(9^2 + 8^2 + \dots + 1^2) = 57$ basis points of
risk on 10 lots, against impact of at most 20. The desk front-loads (3 lots first) because risk outweighs impact here; the
optimum front-loads more when liquidity is deep.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:4}
SARSA learns the value of the \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} it follows, exploration included, so its greedy \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} avoids actions that are costly
when exploration goes wrong; early on that caution helps. \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:td}{Q-learning} learns the optimal values directly and, once they are
learned, its greedy \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} is the optimal one; SARSA's remains optimal for an exploring \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy}, a little more cautious than
needed.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:5}
An episode's weight is a product of ten ratios. With $k$ possible actions, a logged action that the target prefers and
the desk does not has ratio $(0.9 + 0.1/k)/(0.3/k)\approx3k$; one the desk prefers and the target does not has ratio
$(0.1/k)/(0.7 + 0.3/k)$, far below one. On 2\,000 logged episodes the
median weight is $2.8\times10^{-5}$, the 99th percentile 1.56 and the largest 797: one episode carries 78\% of the total
weight. The estimate is effectively an average over a handful of episodes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:6}
The $t$-statistic measures the simulator's noise, not the simulator's error: a year of simulation can be made as long as one
likes. The question is whether the simulator is right in the respects the agent exploits. Evaluate the agent on real logs
(\omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:ope}{off-policy evaluation}), in simulators with different assumptions, and in a limited live trial against the incumbent.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:7}
With the drain the optimal cost rises from 2.59 to 3.01 basis points per lot, and the first sale in thin liquidity rises
from 2 to 3 lots (5 in deep liquidity, unchanged). Selling now makes later periods likelier to be thin, so waiting is worth
less; the optimum sells more while it can.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:8}
One step: $\hat V_{\mathrm{DR}} = \hat V(x) + w(g - \hat Q(x, u))$ with $w = \pi_e(u\mid x)/\pi_b(u\mid x)$. Taking
expectations under $\pi_b$ and the \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{reward}'s distribution, $\E[w(g - \hat Q(x, u))] = \sum_u\pi_e(u\mid x)(Q^{\pi_e}(x, u) -
\hat Q(x, u))$, which is zero when $\hat Q = Q^{\pi_e}$, leaving $\hat V(x) = V^{\pi_e}(x)$. When instead the weights are
right and $\hat Q$ is wrong, the same expression equals $V^{\pi_e}(x) - \hat V(x)$ and the sum is again $V^{\pi_e}(x)$:
unbiased if either part is right.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:1}
\textbf{Part I.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item States (period, lots left, liquidity); actions: lots sold; \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{reward}: minus impact $\eta u^2$ minus risk $0.2q^2$;
liquidity persists with probability 0.8; everything left is sold at the end.
\item 2.59 basis points per lot; 5 lots first in deep liquidity, 2 in thin.
\item 3.27 and 6.76.
\item The state is tiny and the transition probabilities are known; a market has continuous prices, unknown dynamics and
states it never reveals.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Part II.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item At 1\,000 and 10\,000 episodes: \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:td}{Q-learning} 1.41 and 0.044, SARSA 0.28 and 0.051, REINFORCE 0.31 and 0.067, actor-critic
0.11 and 0.009 basis points per lot.
\item Large steps push the softmax towards one action before its value is well estimated; the \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} then stops sampling the
alternatives and cannot learn they are better.
\item It updates at every step from the temporal-difference error, with lower variance than the whole-episode return.
\item At twenty parent orders a day, 500 trading days: two years of a desk's orders for a problem with 242 states.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Part III.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item $\varepsilon$-greedy 0.1: 1\,727; 0.01: 2\,026; UCB with $c = \sqrt2$: 1\,325; with $c = 1$: 944 basis points. UCB with
$c = 1$ is best.
\item It explores so little that in some runs it settles on a good-looking wrong arm and rarely revisits the others.
\item Bias and standard deviation: importance sampling 0.20 and 1.75; weighted 0.49 and 0.63; model alone 0.30 and 0; doubly
robust $-0.03$ and 0.29.
\item The state, the action and the probability with which the behaviour \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} chose it, for every decision.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Part IV.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \emph{Learning to sell.} After 10\,000 episodes \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:td}{Q-learning}'s greedy \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} is 0.044 basis points per lot from the
optimum (2.59). \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{Off-policy} estimates of a \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy} costing 2.77: importance sampling biased by 0.20 with spread 1.75, weighted
importance sampling 0.49 and 0.63, doubly robust $-0.03$ and 0.29.
\item To wait out thin liquidity, which the simulator always ended the next period; in the market it cost 3.83 against the
desk's 3.27, after 2.77 against 3.66 in the simulator.
\item By evaluating in a simulator with persistent liquidity, by \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:ope}{off-policy evaluation} on real logs, or by checking the
simulator's liquidity process against data.
\item When actions do not change future states (routing one order among algorithms), and feedback is immediate.
\item It uses no assumption about liquidity, so an error in that assumption cannot hurt it.
\item Tables become function approximators, exploration becomes costly, and exact evaluation disappears; evaluation must
rest on logs and several simulators.
\item A test bed for mechanisms and for bugs, and a place to compare policies under explicit assumptions, never the final
judge.
\item The environment it was trained in, including its errors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:1}
$Q^*(x, u) = \E[g_{t+1} + \gamma\max_{u'}Q^*(x_{t+1}, u')\mid x_t = x, u_t = u]$: the immediate \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{reward}, plus the discounted
value of acting optimally from the next state, averaged over transitions.

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:2}
\omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:td}{Q-learning}'s target uses the best next action (\omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{off-policy}, learns optimal values); SARSA's uses the next action taken
(\omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{on-policy}, learns the exploring \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy}'s values). It matters when exploration is costly: SARSA learns to avoid states where
an exploratory action is dangerous.
\iqlookfor{off- versus \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{on-policy} targets and a case where they differ.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:3}
\omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:ope}{Off-policy evaluation} from logs with the behaviour \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy}'s probabilities: importance sampling, weighted, or doubly robust with
a model of fills and impact; check support (the new \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{policy}'s actions must have been logged), report the spread, and confirm
with a small live test.
\iqlookfor{importance weighting, support, and a model-based correction.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:4}
A \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:bandit}{contextual bandit}: the context is the order and market state, the \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{reward} the cost against a benchmark, the rule UCB or
Thompson sampling with a \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{reward} model per broker, with minimum allocations for learning and logged probabilities for later
evaluation.
\iqlookfor{bandit framing, exploration rule, and logging.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:5}
$\nabla_\theta J = \E_\pi[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(u_t\mid x_t)(G_t - b(x_t))]$; subtracting a baseline that does
not depend on the action leaves the expectation unchanged, because $\E[\nabla\log\pi] = 0$, and lowers the variance when $b$
is close to the value.
\iqlookfor{the theorem and why the baseline is unbiased.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:6}
Simulator errors it exploits (fills, queue position, impact decay, liquidity persistence, other agents' reactions), latency,
non-stationarity, and rare states. Test in simulators varying each assumption, evaluate on real logs \omterm{def:ml:reinforcement-learning-foundations:mdp}{off-policy}, cap its
actions, and run it small against the incumbent with a kill switch.
\iqlookfor{simulator exploitation and a staged validation.}
\end{solution}
