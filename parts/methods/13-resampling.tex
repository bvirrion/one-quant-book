\chapter{Resampling}\label{ch:qm:resampling}

A strategy that sells volatility has earned a \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio} of 1.1 over five years, and the head of desk asks for a 95\% interval
before doubling its capital. Resampling the five years day by day gives an interval from 0.17 to 2.25: comfortably positive.
Resampling blocks of about a month, which keeps together the quiet weeks and the violent ones, gives $-0.25$ to $2.96$: the data
cannot exclude that the strategy has no edge. Only the second interval is honest, and even it is too narrow. This chapter is about
estimating the uncertainty of a statistic by recomputing it on the data themselves: the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} and why it works, the block
versions that dependent data need, \omterm{def:qm:resampling:perm}{permutation tests} and the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:perm}{exchangeability} that makes them exact, the older \omterm{def:qm:resampling:jackknife}{jackknife}, and the
\omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} version of chapter~12's test for the best of many strategies.

\section{The bootstrap}

\begin{definition}[Empirical distribution function, bootstrap]\label{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}
The \emph{empirical distribution function}\index{empirical distribution function} of $X_1, \dots, X_n$ is $F_n(x) = \frac1n\#\{i: X_i
\le x\}$, the law that puts mass $1/n$ on each observation. The \emph{bootstrap}\index{bootstrap} estimates the sampling law of a
statistic $\hat\theta = T(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ by the law of $\hat\theta^* = T(X_1^*, \dots, X_n^*)$, where the $X_i^*$ are drawn
independently from $F_n$, that is, with replacement from the data; in practice by computing $\hat\theta^*$ on $B$ resamples.
\end{definition}

The principle is a substitution: the unknown law $F$ is replaced by $F_n$, and the sampling experiment that cannot be repeated in
the world is repeated on the computer. The \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error} of a mean is $s\sqrt{(n - 1)/n}/\sqrt n$, the usual one; its use
is for statistics with no formula, or with a formula resting on assumptions one does not trust.

\begin{definition}[Bootstrap percentile interval, coverage probability]\label{def:qm:resampling:interval}
The \emph{bootstrap percentile interval}\index{bootstrap percentile interval} at level $1 - \alpha$ runs from the $\alpha/2$ to the $1 -
\alpha/2$ quantile of the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} draws $\hat\theta^*$. The \emph{coverage probability}\index{coverage probability} of an interval
procedure is the probability, over repeated samples from the true law, that its interval contains the true parameter.
\end{definition}

A nominal 95\% interval is a claim about coverage, and coverage can be measured by simulation: draw many histories from a known
law, build the interval on each, count. That is the test every interval in this chapter is put to.

The \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} is consistent (the law of $\sqrt n(\hat\theta^* - \hat\theta)$ given the data approaches that of $\sqrt n(\hat\theta -
\theta)$) for smooth functionals of $F$ with iid data: means, variances, correlations, \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratios}, regression coefficients. It fails
where the statistic depends on the extremes. For the maximum of a uniform sample, each resample misses the sample maximum with
probability $(1 - 1/n)^n \to e^{-1}$, so the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} law of $n(\max^* - \max)$ has an atom of mass $1 - e^{-1} = 0.632$ at zero,
while the true law of $n(\theta - \max)$ is continuous. Tail quantiles, drawdowns and the largest loss share the problem: the
\omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} cannot resample a crash the history does not contain.

\section{Dependent data: block and stationary bootstraps}

Resampling days independently destroys whatever links one day to the next. For a strategy whose P\&L is serially correlated, the
iid \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} then estimates the variance of the mean by $\hat\gamma(0)/n$ where the truth is the long-run variance over $n$
(chapter~11), and its intervals are too narrow.

\begin{definition}[Moving-block bootstrap, stationary bootstrap]\label{def:qm:resampling:blocks}
The \emph{moving-block bootstrap}\index{moving-block bootstrap} (Künsch, 1989) builds a resample by concatenating blocks of $b$
consecutive observations, $X_{j+1}, \dots, X_{j+b}$, with starting points $j$ drawn uniformly (indices taken modulo $n$ in the
circular version). The \emph{stationary bootstrap}\index{stationary bootstrap} (Politis and Romano, 1994) draws the block lengths
independently from a geometric law of mean $b$: each resampled day continues the current block with probability $1 - 1/b$ and
starts a new block at a uniform position with probability $1/b$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[The stationary bootstrap is stationary]\label{prop:qm:resampling:stationary}
Conditionally on the data, the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{stationary-bootstrap} series $X_1^*, X_2^*, \dots$ is strictly stationary.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $I_t$ be the index of $X_t^*$. $I_1$ is uniform on $\{1, \dots, n\}$. Given $I_{t-1}$, $I_t$ is $I_{t-1} + 1$ (modulo $n$) with
probability $1 - 1/b$ and uniform with probability $1/b$; both maps send the uniform law to itself, so every $I_t$ is uniform. The
sequence $(I_t)$ is a \omterm{def:qm:markov-chains-and-queues:ctmc}{Markov chain} started in its stationary law, hence strictly stationary, and so is $X_t^* = X_{I_t}$.
\end{proof}

The fixed-length blocks of the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:blocks}{moving-block bootstrap} make the resample non-stationary: the joins between blocks fall at fixed
dates. The random lengths remove that artefact at the price of slightly more variance, and both need a block length. Too short, and
the dependence is cut; too long, and there are few distinct blocks to recombine. The mean squared error of the variance estimate is
minimised by a block length growing like $n^{1/3}$, with a constant that depends on the autocovariances; Politis and White (2004),
with the correction of Patton, Politis and White (2009), estimate it from the sample autocovariances with a flat-top lag window.
That is the rule \texttt{optimal\_block\_length} implements.

\begin{example}[The volatility seller]\label{ex:qm:resampling:seller}
Each day the strategy earns a premium and pays the day's squared return, $x_t = \mu + 1 - r_t^2$, with $r_t = \sigma_t\varepsilon_t$ and
$\ln\sigma_t$ an AR(1) with coefficient 0.9 and stationary standard deviation 0.4, scaled so that $\E[\sigma_t^2] = 1$. The market
returns $r_t$ are uncorrelated, but the squared returns are not: $\Cov(r_t^2, r_{t+k}^2) = e^{0.64 \cdot 0.9^k} - 1$, and the P\&L's
long-run variance is 3.9 times its variance. The premium $\mu$ is set for a population \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio} of 1.1. The seeded five-year
history has a \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio} of 1.11, a skewness of $-4.7$, a kurtosis of 34, and autocorrelations of 0.13 at one day, 0.10 at five,
$-0.02$ at twenty.
\end{example}

The automatic rule chooses a mean block of 18.6 days for the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:blocks}{stationary bootstrap} on this history (21.2 for the circular one), and
the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} distributions separate (\cref{fig:qm:resampling:dist}): the iid \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} gives a \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error} of 0.53 and the
interval $(0.17, 2.25)$; the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:blocks}{moving-block bootstrap} 0.84 and $(-0.34, 2.93)$; the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:blocks}{stationary bootstrap} 0.83 and $(-0.25, 2.96)$.
The \omterm{met:qm:estimation:delta}{delta method} of chapter~11 with the sample skewness and kurtosis agrees with the iid \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} (0.52): both assume independent
days.

\section{Permutation tests}

\begin{definition}[Exchangeability, permutation test]\label{def:qm:resampling:perm}
Random variables $Y_1, \dots, Y_n$ are exchangeable (\emph{exchangeability}\index{exchangeability}) if the law of $(Y_{\pi(1)}, \dots,
Y_{\pi(n)})$ is the same for every permutation $\pi$. A \emph{permutation test}\index{permutation test} of the null that $Y$ is
independent of $X$ compares a statistic $T(X, Y)$ with its values over random permutations of $Y$; the $p$-value is $(1 + \#\{T(X,
Y_\pi) \ge T(X, Y)\})/(1 + P)$ for $P$ permutations.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Exactness under exchangeability]\label{prop:qm:resampling:exact}
If, under the null, $Y$ is exchangeable and independent of $X$, the permutation $p$-value satisfies $\P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha$ for
every $\alpha$ and every sample size.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under the null, the observed $Y$ and the $P$ permuted copies are exchangeable given $X$, so the rank of $T(X, Y)$ among the $P + 1$
values is uniform (ties broken against rejection). The $p$-value is that rank from the top divided by $P + 1$, and $\P(\text{rank} \le
k) \le k/(P + 1)$.
\end{proof}

The condition is the whole test. Daily P\&L is not exchangeable when it is serially dependent, and a persistent signal is not either.
Take the volatility seller's P\&L and a signal built from a different, independent market: its trailing twenty-day mean squared return.
Nothing links them. Yet over 200 such independent pairs, the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:perm}{permutation test} of their correlation rejects at 5\% in 21.5\% of them,
because permuting destroys the persistence that makes spurious correlations between two slow series large. Rotating one series by a
random offset instead (a circular shift, which keeps its dependence) rejects in 2.5\%. The same trap catches tests of a signal against
overlapping returns, and the cure is the same: resample in a way that preserves what the null does not break.

\section{The jackknife}

\begin{definition}[Jackknife]\label{def:qm:resampling:jackknife}
The \emph{jackknife}\index{jackknife} recomputes a statistic with each observation left out in turn, $\hat\theta_{(i)}$, and with $\bar\theta
= \frac1n\sum_i\hat\theta_{(i)}$ estimates its bias by $(n - 1)(\bar\theta - \hat\theta)$ and its \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error} by
$\sqrt{\frac{n-1}n\sum_i(\hat\theta_{(i)} - \bar\theta)^2}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Jackknife bias correction]\label{prop:qm:resampling:jack}
If $\E[\hat\theta_n] = \theta + a/n + O(n^{-2})$, the bias-corrected estimate $\hat\theta - (n - 1)(\bar\theta - \hat\theta)$ has bias
$O(n^{-2})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\E[\hat\theta_n] = \theta + a/n + c/n^2 + O(n^{-3})$. Each leave-one-out estimate uses $n - 1$ observations, so $\E[\bar\theta] =
\theta + a/(n - 1) + c/(n - 1)^2 + O(n^{-3})$. The corrected estimate is $n\hat\theta - (n - 1)\bar\theta$, with mean $\theta + a - a +
c\bigl(1/n - 1/(n - 1)\bigr) + O(n^{-2}) = \theta + O(n^{-2})$: the $1/n$ terms cancel exactly.
\end{proof}

For the plug-in variance $\frac1n\sum(X_i - \bar X)^2$, whose bias is exactly $-\sigma^2/n$, the correction returns the unbiased $s^2$
exactly. On the volatility seller's history the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:jackknife}{jackknife} \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error} of the \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio} is 0.53, the iid \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap}'s number, and
for the same reason: leaving out single days, it treats them as independent. A block \omterm{def:qm:resampling:jackknife}{jackknife}, which deletes blocks, is its dependent
version. The \omterm{def:qm:resampling:jackknife}{jackknife} is cheap ($n$ recomputations, no randomness) and good for bias; the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} gives whole distributions.

\section{Bootstrapping the reality check}

Chapter~12's max-statistic test simulated the null of two hundred momentum rules from a Gaussian law with their estimated correlation.
The \omterm{def:qm:testing-and-multiple-testing:rc}{Reality Check} does the same with the data: resample days jointly for all two hundred rules (so that the correlation between rules is
kept), with a \omterm{def:qm:resampling:blocks}{stationary bootstrap} (so that any dependence over time is kept), centre each rule's resampled mean on its sample mean (so
that the resamples obey the null), and take the maximum $t$-statistic of each resample.

With 2\,000 \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{stationary-bootstrap} resamples, the momentum rules' P\&L being nearly independent over time (the automatic block length is
1.3 days), the best rule's adjusted $p$-value is 0.0275, against 0.030 from the Gaussian simulation, and the 5\% critical value of the
maximum is 2.85 against 2.92 (\cref{fig:qm:resampling:rc}). The Gaussian shortcut was adequate here; with fat-tailed or serially
dependent P\&L it would not be, and the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} needs no change.

\end{exercise}

\end{exercise}

\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[$\star\star$]\label{exo:qm:resampling:5}

\section{Problem: The Interval That Was Too Narrow}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- a Sharpe ratio interval for a volatility seller}]\label{pb:qm:resampling:1}
A strategy earns a premium each day and pays the day's squared market return; the market's log volatility follows an AR(1) with
coefficient 0.9 and standard deviation 0.4. The population \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio} is 1.1, and five years of daily P\&L are the chapter's seeded
history.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The history.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item What are the sample \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio}, skewness and kurtosis?
\item What are the autocorrelations at one, five and twenty days?
\item Why is the P\&L autocorrelated when the market's returns are not?
\item What is the population long-run variance of the P\&L, in multiples of its variance?
\item What \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error} does the \omterm{met:qm:estimation:delta}{delta method} give, using the sample skewness and kurtosis?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- Three intervals.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item What are the iid \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{bootstrap} interval and \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error}?
\item What block lengths does the automatic rule choose?
\item What are the moving-block interval and \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error}?
\item What are the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{stationary-bootstrap} interval and \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error}?
\item What do the \omterm{def:qm:resampling:jackknife}{jackknife} \omterm{def:qm:estimation:estimator}{standard error} and bias give?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- Coverage.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item What is the true standard deviation of the five-year \omterm{def:qm:estimation:sharpe}{Sharpe ratio}?
\item How often do iid intervals contain 1.1, over 400 histories?
\item And stationary intervals, with the automatic length and with 40-day blocks?
\item Why does no block length reach 95\%?
\item What happens to the iid interval without volatility clustering?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- Judgement.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item What interval do you give the head of desk?
\item Should the capital be doubled on this evidence?
\item What would you add to the analysis before deciding?
\item State the \emph{named result}: the coverage of the nominal 95\% iid and \omterm{def:qm:resampling:bootstrap}{stationary-bootstrap} intervals.
\item In one sentence: what must a resampling scheme preserve?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
