\chapter{Quantitative Hedge Funds and Systematic Managers}\label{ch:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers}

The most secretive funds in the business still file one public form. Every investment adviser registered with the SEC
answers, at least once a year and under penalty of law, the same questions: how many people it employs, how many of
them do investment work, how much it manages, and how many private funds it runs, with their gross assets. The SEC
publishes the answers as a single file, one row per adviser. In January 2026 that file held 2\,577 advisers that run
hedge funds. Read together, their rows make firms that publish nothing else comparable: the median one employs eleven
people, and among those with ten or more staff the middle half manage between \$58 million and \$221 million per
employee. This chapter reads the quantitative managers among them.

\section{Three families}

Quantitative investment managers are usually described in three families, which differ in method, horizon and the
kind of research job they offer.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Research-driven multi-strategy funds} run many signals across asset classes and horizons, often
alongside discretionary teams, on large research platforms.
\item \textbf{Trend followers}, the commodity trading advisors of Book~1 (chapter~3), trade futures on the persistence
of price moves (Book~8, chapter~19), at horizons of weeks to months.
\item \textbf{Statistical-arbitrage houses} trade large, market-neutral books of shares on short-horizon relative
signals (Book~8, chapter~1).
\end{itemize}
The families overlap, and firms do not always use these words for themselves. What separates the managers of this
chapter from others is how decisions are made.

\begin{definition}[Systematic manager, discretionary manager]\label{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:sys}
A \emph{systematic manager}\index{systematic manager} is an investment manager whose trading decisions are taken by rules
implemented in software, from models fitted to data; people design, test, monitor and change the rules, not the
trades. A \emph{discretionary manager}\index{discretionary manager} is one whose portfolio managers decide the trades,
with whatever analysis supports them.
\end{definition}

The managers named in this chapter describe themselves in those terms: an investment firm that ``employs mathematical
and statistical methods in the design and execution of its investment programs'', ``a quantitative investment and trading
firm'', ``quantitative investors'' for whom ``a systematic and disciplined approach is the best way'', ``one of the
longest running \omterm{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:sys}{systematic managers}'', a firm that approaches investment ``through the prism of machine learning''.
One says it ``launched in 1988 as a pioneer in systematic investing'' and then built fundamental and discretionary
expertise: many large firms are both.

\section{What Form ADV reveals}

\begin{definition}[Regulatory assets under management]\label{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:raum}
An adviser's \emph{regulatory assets under management}\index{regulatory assets under management} (RAUM) are the securities
portfolios over which it provides continuous and regular supervisory or management services, at their gross value: the
adviser does not deduct any outstanding indebtedness or other liabilities of the portfolios.
\end{definition}

RAUM is not the assets under management of Book~1 (chapter~1) as funds report them to investors. It counts gross
positions, so a fund that borrows or holds derivatives reports more than its net asset value; and it counts only
securities portfolios. A manager with modest capital and high gross leverage has large RAUM. For a job seeker the gap
matters when comparing firms: RAUM measures what the adviser runs, not what investors have entrusted.

\begin{definition}[Exempt reporting adviser]\label{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:era}
An \emph{exempt reporting adviser}\index{exempt reporting adviser} is an investment adviser exempt from SEC registration --
for example because it advises only private funds and manages less than \$150 million of private-fund assets in the
United States -- that must still file a short report on Form ADV.
\end{definition}

Four items of the form carry most of what a reader wants:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{5.A} the approximate number of employees;
\item \textbf{5.B(1)} how many of them ``perform investment advisory functions (including research)'';
\item \textbf{5.F} \omterm{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:raum}{regulatory assets under management};
\item \textbf{7.B} the private funds advised: whether any is a hedge fund, how many, and their total gross assets.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[What the form does not show]\label{rem:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:limits}
The form describes one registered adviser. A group can split its staff and assets across several advisers and
affiliates, some registered elsewhere: the US adviser of a global firm may employ a few dozen of its thousands of
staff. The form does not report returns, fees, pay or revenue. And answers are updated once a year at least, so a row
may be most of a year old.
\end{remark}

\section{Assets, gross assets and concentration}

Among the 1\,458 hedge-fund advisers with ten or more employees in the January 2026 file, RAUM per employee has a
median of \$119 million, a middle half from \$58 million to \$221 million, and a tenth of advisers above \$399 million
(\cref{fig:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:hist}). Half of a typical adviser's staff do investment
work (the quartiles of the advisory share are 37\% and 62\%). The industry is not concentrated: the ten largest
advisers by private-fund gross assets hold 21\% of the \$17.6 trillion reported by all hedge-fund advisers, and the
Herfindahl--Hirschman index (Book~16, chapter~30) of those gross assets is 78 on a scale of 10\,000.

\begin{method}[Reading a manager's Form ADV]\label{met:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:read}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Find every adviser of the group (the file's legal names, the firm's own disclosures) and read their rows
separately.
\item Compare RAUM with private-fund gross assets: a ratio far below one means managed accounts or registered funds
outside the private funds; far above one, private funds counted in another adviser's RAUM.
\item Read the advisory share (5.B(1) over 5.A) as the share of staff in research and portfolio work.
\item Check the filing date; treat every figure as a dated range.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Secrecy and publication}

\omterm{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:sys}{Systematic managers} are secretive about their models and open about their methods. What they publish falls into three
kinds: research papers and commentary on factors, trends and risk premia, written for investors; academic papers by
their researchers, often on statistics and machine learning; and descriptions of method on their own pages, such as one
firm's statement that it uses ``scientific and academic methods including the latest machine learning techniques to find
trading signals in global financial and alternative data''. None of this reveals a signal. It tells a candidate what
the firm values and what kind of research job it offers: a manager that publishes factor research hires researchers who
write; one that publishes nothing but a sentence hires people who will not.

\section{Tutorial: assets per head from the SEC's adviser file}\label{tut:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:tut}

\begin{tutorial}
\textbf{Goal.} Compute the distribution of RAUM per employee of hedge-fund advisers and place thirteen named quantitative
advisers in it. \textbf{End state:} \cref{fig:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:hist} and the ranks of
exercise~5.
\begin{tutsteps}
\item \textbf{The file.} Download the SEC's adviser file for a month (about 5\,MB zipped, one CSV of about 16\,600 rows
and 448 columns) to a scratch directory; it is public, and it is not committed.
\item \textbf{Read it.} \texttt{firm.formadv.read(path)} streams the CSV from the zip and keeps, for advisers of hedge
funds, the items of section~2 (\cref{lst:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:read}).
\omcode{code/firm/formadv/firm_formadv.py}{81}{97}{Stream the adviser file and keep hedge-fund advisers.}\label{lst:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:read}
\item \textbf{Derive.} \texttt{in\_adv\_derive.py} writes three small tables to \texttt{data/industry/}: the
distribution statistics, the named advisers' rows, and a histogram; the chapter's figures and tests read only these.
\item \textbf{Place.} \texttt{in\_quantfunds.percentile\_rank(x)} gives the share of advisers with ten or more employees
below a RAUM per employee of $x$.
\end{tutsteps}
\end{tutorial}

\textbf{What to change next.} Repeat with the file of a year earlier and compare the named advisers' employees
(chapter~5 does this for multi-manager platforms); restrict to advisers whose private-fund gross assets exceed their
RAUM and see how the distribution moves.

\section{Build: the adviser file reader}\label{bld:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:formadv}

\begin{build}
\bfield{Purpose} One reader for the SEC's Form ADV file, used by chapters 4, 5, 8 and 12.
\bfield{Interface} \texttt{firm.formadv}: \texttt{COLS}; \texttt{Adviser(crd, name, employees, advisory, raum, pf\_gav,
n\_hedge, filed)}; \texttt{read(path\_or\_fileobj, hedge\_only)}; \texttt{per\_head(a)}; \texttt{quantiles(values,
qs)}; \texttt{concentration(values, k)}, which wraps \texttt{firm.moats}.
\bfield{Rules} Streams the file and never writes raw rows back; a blank answer is \texttt{None}, never zero; per-head
ratios are undefined without a positive headcount.
\bfield{Acceptance tests} \texttt{code/firm/formadv/tests/}: a three-row fixture in the file's own format; the hedge-fund
filter; per-head ratios and a missing advisory count; the top-$k$ share and index on known shares.
\bfield{Stretch} Read the monthly files of several years into a panel keyed by the adviser's CRD number; read Schedule
D's private-fund rows from the full Form ADV data set.
\end{build}

\section{Problem: Assets per Head}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- assets per head}]\label{pb:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:1}
A researcher weighing offers from three \omterm{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:sys}{systematic managers} wants to know how large each is and how research-heavy,
from public records only.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The families and the form.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Define a systematic and a \omterm{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:sys}{discretionary manager}, and describe the three families.
\item Which four items of Form ADV carry most of what a reader wants?
\item Define RAUM and say how it differs from assets under management.
\item Define an \omterm{def:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:era}{exempt reporting adviser} and give the threshold of the private-fund exemption.
\item What three limits of the form does \cref{rem:in:quantitative-hedge-funds-and-systematic-managers:limits} name?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The distribution.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item How many hedge-fund advisers are in the January 2026 file, and how many have ten or more employees?
\item Give the median and the quartiles of RAUM per employee for the latter.
\item Give the quartiles of the advisory share.
\item Give the ten largest advisers' share of private-fund gross assets and the Herfindahl--Hirschman index.
\item What is the median number of employees of a hedge-fund adviser?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The named advisers.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item Compute RAUM per employee for the thirteen named advisers and rank them.
\item Which lie inside the middle half of the distribution?
\item Which have the lowest and the highest advisory share?
\item Which two rows belong to one group, and why must they not be added?
\item Which ratio of RAUM to private-fund gross assets is furthest below one?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The verdict.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item State the \emph{named result}: the middle half of RAUM per employee for hedge-fund advisers, the range across the
named quantitative advisers, and the concentration measures.
\item Why is the largest named ratio a fact about an entity, not a firm?
\item What does an advisory share of 16\% tell a researcher about a firm?
\item What would the researcher need that the form does not report?
\item In two sentences, how should the researcher use the three rows?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
