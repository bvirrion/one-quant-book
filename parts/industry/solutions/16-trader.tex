\begin{solution}{exo:in:trader:4}
When $\lambda\ll\mu$, $P(W>t)\approx(\lambda/\mu)e^{-\mu t}$, so the standard $P\le p$ gives $\lambda\le\mu pe^{\mu t}$, a
fixed total alert rate; with $N$ algorithms at $a$ each, $N\le\mu pe^{\mu t}/a$. Here $\mu pe^{\mu t}=120\times0.01\times
e^{2}=8.9$ alerts an hour: about 8.9 algorithms at one alert each, 7 once the $\lambda$ in the exponent is kept.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:in:trader:5}
The survey's occupation includes brokers, advisers and sales staff across the securities industry, not only traders at

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:in:trader:6}
The waiting time of a single-server queue grows with the variance of the handling time (the Pollaczek--Khinchine
formula): long handlings, which the lognormal makes more frequent, hold up everyone behind them.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:in:trader:7}
With the Erlang C formula, two supervisors sharing one queue meet the standard for 76 algorithms at one alert an hour
(0.99\% of alerts wait over a minute; 1.03\% at 77): far more than twice 7, because an alert waits only when both are busy.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:in:trader:8}
Utilisation of one half means the supervisor is busy half the time, so half the alerts wait, and in an M/M/1 queue
$P(W>60\text{ s})=0.5e^{-1}=18.4\%$, far above a 1\% standard; doubling the load would make the queue unstable.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:in:trader:1}
\begin{enumerate}
\item One who decides positions and owns a book's P\&L (a market maker's options trader); one who executes others'
decisions (a hedge fund's \omterm{def:in:trader:kinds}{execution trader}).
\item Market maker: a \omterm{def:in:trader:kinds}{risk trader} of the quotes' inventory who builds and watches automation; bank flow desk: prices clients
through sales and manages the flow risk; hedge fund: mainly execution for the portfolio managers.
\item One who trades by hand on a screen; one who sets, monitors, handles alerts from and stops trading algorithms.
\item Not to reopen its trading pits except one options pit; 93\%.
\item Real-time monitoring by the trader in charge together with the risk function, and a timely response to operational
and regulatory issues.
\item A rule to assess and certify people in named functions as fit and proper; in the UK, proprietary traders who could
cause significant harm and the people who approve or monitor trading algorithms.
\item Securities Trader Representative, after the SIE and Series~57 examinations.
\item The ability to cancel all or any unexecuted orders at once as an emergency measure; the firm, through its traders and
risk staff, with compliance in contact with them.
\item Pre-open checks, the open, the session's adjustments and alerts, incidents and stops, and the after-close
reconciliation and review.
\item Median offered base \$195\,000 (market makers) and \$235\,000 (banks); the survey's sales agents \$103\,030; bonuses are
in neither.
\item Poisson alerts at one an hour per algorithm, handling of 30 seconds on average, at most 1\% of alerts waiting over a
minute.
\item $\rho e^{-(\mu-\lambda)t}$: 0.89\% at 7, 1.03\% at 8.
\item 15 at 0.5 an hour, 7 at 1, 3 at 2, 1 at 4.
\item 71 at one alert an hour.
\item 1.37\% of alerts wait over a minute at 7 algorithms, so fewer than 7 meet the standard.
\item Seven algorithms at one alert an hour each; 15, 3 and 1 at 0.5, 2 and 4 alerts an hour.
\item Fewer alerts per algorithm (and a shared queue with a colleague).
\item The waiting time grows much faster than utilisation near full load, and a busy fraction says nothing of the tail.
\item The real alert rate and its peaks, the handling-time distribution, and the cost of a late response.
\item About seven algorithms per trader at an alert an hour, if a minute is the standard; spend first on better alerts and on
pooling traders on one queue, which multiply that number far more than hiring does.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:in:trader:1}
Stop it (or reduce it to flat) first, then understand: check positions, recent fills, market data and parameter changes;
tell risk and the desk head; restart only when the cause is known.
\iqlookfor{stopping before diagnosing, and escalation.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:in:trader:2}
A kill switch cancels orders at once on command; a loss limit stops trading automatically when losses reach a threshold.
One is a tool for a person, the other a rule.
\iqlookfor{manual emergency action against automatic threshold.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:in:trader:3}
$\rho=60\times30/3600=0.5$; mean wait $\rho/(\mu-\lambda)=0.5/(120-60)$ hours, 30 seconds.
\iqlookfor{utilisation and the M/M/1 mean wait.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:in:trader:4}
Page on anything that threatens money or orderliness now: position or loss limits near, fills without matching hedges,
stale market data, rejected orders at a venue, quoting outside expected spreads. Log slow drifts and one-off
parameter warnings, and review them after the close.
\iqlookfor{urgency and actionability as the criteria.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:in:trader:5}
Widen for the event risk and the size, hedge part before and the rest after if the client allows, and agree with sales
what the client is told; never trade ahead of the client.
\iqlookfor{event risk, size, and conduct.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:in:trader:6}
Compare the algorithms' results in comparable periods with and without the trader's interventions, or measure each
intervention's effect on the following P\&L and risk; judge the average effect net of the interventions that hurt.
\iqlookfor{a counterfactual, not anecdotes.}
\end{solution}
