\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows} --- Zero-Day Options and Dealer-Gamma Flows}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:1}
Their options' delta changes by $-5 \times 2 = -10$ index units; to stay hedged they buy 10 units, after the rise.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:2}
Gamma at the money is $\varphi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt\tau)$ with $d_1 \to 0$: it grows like $1/\sqrt\tau$. With little time left, a small move decides
whether the option ends in or out of the money, so its delta swings from near 0 to near 1.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:3}
Round strikes are 0.5 apart and the window is 0.05 on either side, 0.1 of every 0.5: for a close spread evenly over a range much wider than 0.5, the
share is $0.1/0.5 = 20\%$.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:4}
The convention puts dealers long all calls, which sit above the open, and short all puts, which sit below: gamma is negative below the open and
positive above by construction, so a sign change near the open is built into the assumption rather than found in the data.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:5}
The average sides are the same every day; what decides a day's dealer gamma is that day's customer sides, which vary with a standard deviation of
0.5 around the average. Open interest carries no information about them.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:6}
Short-gamma dealers buy after rises and sell after falls, adding to moves (1.48\% a day against 0.99\% without feedback); long-gamma dealers lean
against moves (0.81\%). Ni and co-authors found this noninformational channel in single stocks: hedge rebalancing raised volatility and the
probability of large moves.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:7}
Nothing: without impact the index is a random walk. The rule by true regime earns 0.03 basis points a day ($t$ = 0.05). The unconditional trade shows
$-1.38$ with $t = -2.2$ on these 2\,000 days, a reminder that a $t$ of 2 appears by chance in a series whose true mean is zero.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:8}
The estimate's sign agrees with the truth on only 56\% of days, and the trade conditioned on it earns 1.02 basis points on estimated short-gamma days
($t$ = 1.0) against 3.12 on true ones. The statement treats an assumption about sides as a measurement.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:1}
\begin{enumerate}
\item An option on its expiry day; its gamma near the strike grows like $1/\sqrt\tau$ towards the close.
\item 2.3 million contracts a day, 59\% of SPX volume in 2025; a record 62.4\% in August 2025.
\item Short gamma: buy after rises, sell after falls; long gamma: the reverse.
\item Market makers' hedge rebalancing raises stock volatility and the probability of large moves, with no information behind it.
\item Dealer gamma from open interest and an assumed side; the level where that estimate changes sign.
\item Dealers long every call and short every put: customers sell calls and buy puts.
\item On 56.0\% of days, with a correlation of 0.23.
\item The convention on 96.0\% of the first 200 days, the truth on 36.5\%.
\item A tenth of the unhedged delta every five minutes, the rest in the last 30 minutes; each trade moves the next bar.
\item 1.48\% on short-gamma days, 0.81\% on long-gamma days, 0.99\% without feedback.
\item A close drawn to a large strike by long-gamma hedging; no: 21.4\% of closes near a round strike on long-gamma days, 20\% by chance.
\item Gamma concentrated at one strike late in the day and hedging that tracks each option's delta closely.
\item \textbf{Named result.} The conventional gamma estimate gets the sign wrong on 44\% of days (correlation 0.23 with the truth); the
last-30-minute trade earns 3.12 basis points a day on true short-gamma days and $-3.40$ on long ones, a rule using the true side earns a Sharpe ratio
of 1.68, and the same rule on the estimate 0.40 before costs and $-0.11$ after.
\item The last 30 minutes' return is predicted by the rest of the day's, in more than 60 futures markets, linked to gamma hedging.
\item 0.27 basis points a day, a Sharpe ratio of 0.14.
\item The rule with the true side (1.16 after costs); on the estimate, nothing.
\item Trade-level data with initiator flags, dealer surveys, or exchange data on customer and market-maker volume.
\item Open interest as known before the session, costs every day, the regime estimate made that morning.
\item Long gamma below the flip level.
\item On knowing which side of the options the dealers hold.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:1}
Their options lose delta as the index rises and gain it as it falls, so to stay hedged they buy after rises and sell after falls. Their trades add to
the move, more so when zero-day gamma is large late in the day.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:2}
Sum open interest times gamma by strike under an assumption about sides. Test it against anything that reveals the truth: signed volume by
participant type, the realised volatility and autocorrelation of intraday returns by estimated regime, and the stability of the estimate's sign.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:3}
If dealers are long gamma there, the index may be held near it; if short, a break away may accelerate. Trade small, with defined risk (butterflies
rather than naked straddles), and only if the history shows \omterm{def:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:pinning}{pinning} beyond chance.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:4}
Large gamma near the close: a move of a few points changes the hedge sharply and a gap cannot be hedged. Limit by strike concentration, by the loss
on a move of several points in the last hour, and by hedging capacity near the close.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:5}
Open interest from the morning's file, intraday volume by strike, live quotes for implied vols, a gamma per strike on a grid of index levels, the side
assumption as a configuration, and alerts when the estimate's sign or flip level moves; with checks on stale quotes.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s2:zero-day-options-and-dealer-gamma-flows:6}
At the money with zero rates, $d_1 = \tfrac12\sigma\sqrt\tau$, and $\Gamma = \varphi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt\tau)$. As $\tau \to 0$, $\varphi(d_1) \to
1/\sqrt{2\pi}$, so $\Gamma \approx 1/(S\sigma\sqrt{2\pi\tau})$: it grows like $\tau^{-1/2}$.
\end{solution}
