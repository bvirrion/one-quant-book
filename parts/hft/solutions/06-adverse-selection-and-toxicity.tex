\begin{solution}{exo:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:4}
Every change of a side's state cancels an order and every return sends a new one; the base quoter only reprices when the best price moves.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:5}
In a one-tick book the next price move happens when a best queue is emptied; a side holding a small share of the two queues is the one about to be
emptied, which is when informed traders are taking it. The other features are weaker versions of the same information.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:6}

\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:7}
With hysteresis: 716 messages a session against 952, 9,767 shares against 10,733, an informed share of 21.2\% against 17.9\%, a ten-second mark-out
of 0.102. It saves a quarter of the messages but does not lower toxicity: a side kept out longer also misses the good moments and returns at the back
of its queue.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:8}
VPIN's predictive content comes largely from trading intensity (Andersen and Bondarenko), and a market-wide stress metric says nothing about which
of one's own quotes will be picked off. Widening everywhere gives up the good flow with the bad; score one's own fills, side by side, and act on the
sides the score flags.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:1}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Anonymous exchange: venue, order type, size, time of day, book state; request for quote: the counterparty itself.
\item Limit orders routed to venues paying large rebates were executed worse on several quality measures, adverse selection among them.
\item Informed $-0.95$ (se 0.14), uninformed $+0.55$ (0.10) ticks a share at ten seconds; 32\% of fills informed.
\item Informed fills are a third of the fills and lose almost twice what the others earn: $+0.07$ in total.
\item An estimate of flow toxicity from order imbalance in volume time; a poor volatility predictor, highest only after the flash crash, its content
mechanical in trading intensity.
\item See \cref{def:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:score}; flow against the side, the other side's queue share, quiet time, a wide spread.
\item $1.43-0.029\,\text{flow}-1.79\,\text{share}-0.072\ln(1+\text{quiet})+0.028\,\text{wide}$; 0.19 in sample, 0.18 out of sample.
\item Realised mark-outs rise across fifths, from $-0.86$ to $+0.83$: the score orders fills correctly out of sample.
\item See \cref{def:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:widening}; fading withdraws, skewing moves both quotes, refusing prices out or rejects a known
counterparty.
\item Fading and widening by a tick.
\item None: 19,167 shares, 29.4\%, $-0.005$; fade: 6,550, 18.3\%, 0.136; widen: 6,683, 20.0\%, 0.112.
\item The mark-outs' standard errors (0.10 to 0.22) are as large as the differences.
\item Informed share from 29.4\% to 17.9\%, ten-second mark-out from $-0.005$ to 0.214 ticks a share (standard error 0.146), volume from 19,167 to
10,733 shares a session: 44\% given up.
\item Near zero the score crosses the threshold back and forth; each crossing sends the order to the back of its queue, where fills are worst.
\item The quoter fades too rarely: at 0.4 the informed share is 24.1\%, at 0.6 30.1\%.
\item Fewer informed fills during the news window (23.6\% against 34.7\% before): noise flow rises with the news; toxicity varies over time.
\item Quote it again at a small size or a normal price for a period, mark out its fills, and restore it if they are no worse than the average.
\item The choice of depth returns: widening becomes a continuous response (chapter 4's half the expected move), and fading is rarely needed.
\item Price the counterparty (a wider quote or a lower win rate), or reject under last look.
\item To decide, side by side and moment by moment, where to show liquidity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:1}
Different counterparties (informed flow concentrates on the venue); different fees (rebate venues attract toxic takers, as Battalio, Corwin and
Jennings found); different latency (you are slower on that venue and picked off). Split mark-outs by order type and time, compare fill times with your
latency, and check whether the gap moves with the fee schedule.
\iqlookfor{several hypotheses and a way to separate them.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:2}
Selection: fills happen more when quotes are stale or when the score was low, and quotes you withdrew have no mark-out. Randomise part of the
policy, weight by the probability of being filled, or model the fill and the mark-out jointly.
\iqlookfor{recognising selection and a remedy.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:3}
It can be: mark-outs are very noisy, so a small correlation still sorts fills by a tick or more. What matters is the spread of realised mark-outs
across score buckets out of sample, the volume given up, and the cost of acting (messages, lost queue place).
\iqlookfor{thinking in buckets and costs, not in $R^2$.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:4}
The order is cancelled and re-entered at every crossing, losing its queue place and flooding the gateway. Add hysteresis or a minimum hold, and
count messages per side in the tests.
\iqlookfor{priority loss and hysteresis.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:5}
Its quoting obligation: a minimum time at or near the best prices and maximum widths (One Quant Book 1, chapter 24). It can widen within the
allowed width or reduce size, but not fade for long.
\iqlookfor{obligations as a constraint on the response.}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:hf:adverse-selection-and-toxicity:6}
Remaining fills: informed $\pi(1-a)$, uninformed $(1-\pi)(1-b)$; mean mark-out $\bigl[(1-\pi)(1-b)G-\pi(1-a)L\bigr]/\bigl[\pi(1-a)+(1-\pi)(1-b)\bigr]$,
which improves on $(1-\pi)G-\pi L$ exactly when $a>b$. Volume falls to $\pi(1-a)+(1-\pi)(1-b)$ of its level.
\iqlookfor{the mixture algebra and the condition $a>b$.}
\end{solution}
