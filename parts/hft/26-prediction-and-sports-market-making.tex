\chapter{Prediction and Sports Market Making}\label{ch:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making}

A goal is scored, and the television picture arrives several seconds after the stadium's. The betting exchange holds every in-play order for a few
seconds before it matches, so that the fans in the stands do not win every race. In this chapter's model, a market maker in a match-winner market
whose data feed reports goals three seconds after they happen loses \$236 per goal to courtsiders at the ground when the exchange matches at once;
a three-second \omterm{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:delay}{bet delay} cuts that to \$66, a six-second delay to \$1, and its spread income of \$90 a match becomes a profit.

\section{Pricing models for events and matches}

Prediction markets and betting exchanges (One Quant Book 3, chapter 27) trade contracts that pay if an event happens; their prices are
probabilities. A football match is modelled well enough for a market maker by independent Poisson goals for each side (One Quant Book 4, chapter
6): with rates of 1.5 and 1.1 goals a match, the home side wins with probability 0.464 at kick-off. In play, the probability is recomputed from the
score and the time left: one goal up at half-time, 0.760. Each goal is a jump in the \omterm{def:hf:fair-value:fair}{fair price}, and the whole of the market maker's problem is
the seconds after it (\cref{fig:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:match}).

Croxson and Reade used exactly that clean news, a goal, to test the efficiency of betting prices on high-frequency data: on their evidence, prices
update swiftly and fully. Swiftly is the point: someone must be first.

\section{Making markets on exchanges and prediction venues}

A market maker on an exchange quotes both sides of a contract: it lays the home win at the fair probability plus a half-spread and backs it at the
fair probability less one. At 0.46 with a one-point half-spread, a bettor gets decimal odds of 2.13 on the home win and 1.82 on the other side, a
book with an overround of 2\% (Book 3's \texttt{firm.odds}). On a prediction venue organised as a central limit order book the same quote is a bid
and an offer on a contract priced between 0 and 1. Recreational bettors pay the spread; the chapter's staking twice a minute at 50 gives the market
maker \$90 a match.

\begin{dated}{2026-09}{Where these contracts trade, and the delay}\label{dat:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:venues}
A betting exchange's developer support explains that in-play markets carry a delay on placing bets, varying from 1 to 12 seconds, to allow customers
to cancel unmatched orders when market conditions change. In the United States a federal district court ruled on 12 September 2024 that the CFTC had
exceeded its authority in blocking a designated contract market's election contracts; the CFTC's appeal was voluntarily dismissed on 7 May 2025.
In January 2022 the CFTC settled charges against a crypto prediction market for offering event-based binary options without registration, with a
\$1.4 million penalty (One Quant Book 3, chapter 27).
\end{dated}

\section{In-play latency and the bet delay}

\begin{definition}[Courtsiding]\label{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:courtsiding}
\emph{Courtsiding}\index{courtsiding} is trading on an event from inside the venue where it happens, faster than the official data feed and the
television broadcast carry it, so as to take quotes that do not yet reflect it.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Bet delay]\label{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:delay}
A \emph{bet delay}\index{bet delay} is the time a betting exchange holds an in-play order before it can match, so that market makers can cancel
their quotes after an event before orders placed on knowledge of the event reach them; it is an \omterm{def:hf:latency-arbitrage-and-its-defence:bump}{asymmetric speed bump} (chapter 9) for sport.
\end{definition}

With no delay every goal is sniped and the maker loses \$236 a goal, \$528 a match net of its spread income
(\cref{fig:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:delay}). Delays shorter than the maker's own latency help little: at two seconds, 72\% of goals
are still sniped. At three seconds, 29\% and \$66; at four, 8\% and \$20, and the market pays; at six, \$1 a goal, under 1\% of the no-delay loss.
The delay must exceed the market maker's data latency less the courtsider's, with a margin for the spread of both.

\section{Limits and counterparty selection}

\begin{definition}[Stake limiting]\label{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:limiting}
\emph{Stake limiting}\index{stake limiting} is a bookmaker's or market maker's restriction of the stakes it accepts from an account whose bets have
proved informed, measured by the mark-out of its past bets, leaving its prices open to others.
\end{definition}

A bookmaker sees the account on every bet and can limit it; an exchange market maker sees only anonymous orders, but a venue can offer it tools
(minimum delays by market, caps by account). In the model, capping any one courtsider's stake at 100 units instead of the 1\,000 quoted cuts the loss
per goal from \$236 to \$24, and at 50 units to \$12. \omterm{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:courtsiding}{Courtsiding} itself sits in a legal grey zone: in 2014 Victoria's prosecutors withdrew a
charge of conduct that would corrupt a betting outcome against a man accused of sending live scores from the Australian Open to a betting agency,
because there was no reasonable prospect of a conviction.

\section{Strategy files}

\begin{strategyfile}{Prediction-market making on a central limit order book}\label{strat:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:clob}
\sfield{Who pays you, and why} Traders expressing views on events, and hedgers of event risk, who pay the spread.
\sfield{Instruments and venues} Event contracts on designated contract markets and crypto prediction venues.
\sfield{Signal} A model probability (polls, models, related markets) and the order book's flow.
\sfield{Sizing and execution} Quote both sides around the model; skew with inventory; cap exposure per event; widen near resolution news.
\sfield{Costs} Fees; capital locked until resolution; resolution-source risk.
\sfield{How it dies} Informed flow near news, and regulation: the dated box's court rulings and settlement show how fast the permitted set changes.
\sfield{Horizon, capacity, infrastructure} Hours to months to resolution.
\sfield{Backtest honestly} The venue's book at each time and its fees; resolution as it happened.
\sfield{Sources} One Quant Book 3, chapter 27; the dated box.
\end{strategyfile}

\begin{strategyfile}{In-play exchange trading with the bet delay}\label{strat:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:inplay}
\sfield{Who pays you, and why} Recreational in-play bettors, through the spread; the delay protects the maker from courtsiders.
\sfield{Instruments and venues} Match-odds and other in-play markets on betting exchanges.
\sfield{Signal} An in-play model (Poisson goals for football) updated from an official data feed.
\sfield{Sizing and execution} Quote around the model; cancel on every event from the feed; size by the delay's margin over the feed's latency.
\sfield{Costs} Exchange commission on net winnings; losses when the feed is slower than the delay.
\sfield{How it dies} Delays shorter than the feed's latency: \$236 a goal with no delay, \$66 at three seconds; faster data for courtsiders.
\sfield{Horizon, capacity, infrastructure} Seconds; the fastest official feed available.
\sfield{Backtest honestly} Event timestamps from the ground, the feed's and the exchange's, and the delay by market.
\sfield{Sources} Croxson and Reade (2014); the exchange's developer support (dated box); this chapter.
\end{strategyfile}

\begin{strategyfile}{Cross-venue event arbitrage}\label{strat:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:cross}
\sfield{Who pays you, and why} Traders on the venue whose price lags, or bettors with bookmakers whose overround leaves room.
\sfield{Instruments and venues} The same event on exchanges, bookmakers and prediction venues.
\sfield{Signal} Implied probabilities across venues, net of commissions and overrounds (Book 3's \texttt{firm.odds}).
\sfield{Sizing and execution} Back on one venue and lay on another when the combined odds lock in a profit; small stakes where limiting bites.
\sfield{Costs} Commissions, stake limits, capital on several venues, differing settlement rules.
\sfield{How it dies} \omterm{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:limiting}{Stake limiting} of accounts that win, and differences in how venues settle the same event.
\sfield{Horizon, capacity, infrastructure} Minutes to the event.
\sfield{Backtest honestly} Prices available to the account at the time, commissions, and each venue's rules.
\sfield{Sources} One Quant Book 3, chapter 27.
\end{strategyfile}

\section{Build: the sports market maker}\label{bld:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:sportsmm}

\begin{build}
\bfield{Purpose} Price matches in play, make markets on an exchange with a \omterm{def:hf:prediction-and-sports-market-making:delay}{bet delay}, and limit counterparties.
\bfield{Interface} \texttt{home\_win(lh, la, t\_left, score\_diff)}, \texttt{Match(lh, la, seed)}, \texttt{trade\_match(match, delay\_s, mm\_lat,
fast\_lat, size, half\_spread, rec\_per\_min, rec\_stake, cap, lat\_sd)}, \texttt{season(n, delay\_s, seed)}, \texttt{odds\_quote(p, half\_spread)}.
Built on Book 3's \texttt{firm.odds}.
\bfield{Rules} Probabilities in $[0,1]$; stakes in units paying one; latencies lognormal around their medians.
\bfield{Acceptance tests} \texttt{code/firm/sportsmm/tests/}: home, draw and away sum to one; the end of a match resolves the probability; a goal
raises it; with no delay every goal is sniped, with ten seconds none; a cap scales the loss; the quote's odds and overround by hand.
\bfield{Stretch} Market suspension; red cards; slow and fast feeds among makers.
\end{build}
