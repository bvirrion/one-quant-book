\section*{Chapter \ref{ch:s1:intraday-patterns} --- Intraday Patterns}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:1}
$16\%/\sqrt{20} = 3.6\%$ a year for the whole return; the overnight part has a volatility of $16\% \times \sqrt{0.25} = 8\%$, so $8\%/\sqrt{20} = 1.8\%$ a year.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:2}
A quarter: $0.25 \times 7\% = 1.75\%$ a year overnight, 5.25\% by day.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:3}
$0.6 \times 10 = 6$ basis points per standard deviation of imbalance, before costs; six basis points every day would be $6 \times 252/100 = 15.1\%$ a year. The
model's trade earns 8.8\% because imbalances vary in size, the trade takes a fixed position whatever the imbalance, and it pays a basis point a day.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:4}
$0.05 \times 2\% = 0.10\%$, ten basis points of buying pressure in the last half hour.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:5}
A short-gamma book's delta falls as the price rises (it becomes shorter), so its hedger buys after rises and sells after falls: with the move. A long-gamma
book's delta rises with the price, so its hedger sells into rises and buys into falls: against the move.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:6}
A twenty-year mean return has a standard error of 3.6 points a year and its overnight part 1.8 points: the model's planted quarter was measured as 54\%.
Bondarenko and Muravyev's result concentrates the return in four hours with a Sharpe ratio of 1.6, which is detectable in a way that a split of a noisy
annual mean is not.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:7}
The fade depends only on the closing imbalance and its \omterm{def:s1:short-term-reversal:overnight}{overnight reversal}, which do not depend on the dealers' hedging: 1.09 and 1.08. The last-half-hour
trade exists only when hedging makes the last half hour follow the day: $-0.77$ without it, 1.05 with it.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exo:s1:intraday-patterns:8}
A sample average of overnight minus daytime returns is noisy (the model measures 54\% overnight when a quarter is planted), and holding only overnight
halves the time invested and doubles the trading. Test the split on a long, round-the-clock sample, with costs, and ask why the premium would be earned
at night.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{pb:s1:intraday-patterns:1}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Close to next open; open to close.
\item The entire average E-mini return in four hours around the European open; reversal and momentum profits earned overnight, with cross-period reversal.
\item A quarter planted (1.75\% a year); 54\% measured (4.4\% overnight, 3.7\% by day).
\item Sampling error: a twenty-year mean is too noisy to split.
\item The U-shaped pattern of volume across the day; 12.1\% in each of the first and last half hours, 4.9\% at midday.
\item Before 3:50 p.m. on-close orders; from 3:50 imbalance data; MOC cut-off 3:55, LOC 3:58; the cross at 4:00.
\item \omterm{def:s1:index-rebalancing:fund}{Index funds}, funds pricing at the close, dealers marking their books: the benchmark price is set there.
\item 8.8\% a year at a Sharpe ratio of 1.09.
\item Trading near the close to rebalance exposure after the day's move; with the move when short gamma.
\item The last 30 minutes follow the rest of the day in over sixty futures, 1974--2020, reverting over the next days, linked to gamma hedging.
\item Slope $-0.006$ and a losing trade ($-0.77$) without; 0.044 ($t = 9.2$) and a Sharpe ratio of 1.05 with.
\item The hedge trades against the move and the pattern reverses.
\item \textbf{Named result.} 54\% of the model's cumulative return is measured overnight where a quarter is planted, a sampling error of a twenty-year mean;
the last half hour's slope on the rest of the day is $-0.006$ ($t = -1.2$) without hedging flow and 0.044 ($t = 9.2$) with it.
\item The overnight window is a risk premium; the last-half-hour and imbalance trades are liquidity trades.
\item From option open interest and dealers' likely side, aggregated into net gamma by strike.
\item Spreads and fees on daily round trips; auction fees.
\item The whole clock of futures data, its costs, and the stability of the result across periods.
\item The overnight window in index futures.
\item Reconstitution days concentrate index flow in the closing auction, where the imbalance trade and chapter~10's liquidity provision meet.
\item It is where the benchmark prices are set, so those who must trade at them pay those who can wait.
\end{enumerate}
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s1:intraday-patterns:1}
At the open, overnight news and orders are traded; at the close, benchmark prices are set and funds, index trackers and hedgers trade there; midday has
neither.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s1:intraday-patterns:2}
The last half hour's return tends to follow the day's return up to that point. Hedging by short-gamma dealers and leveraged ETFs, which must trade with the
move near the close, and late-informed trading.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s1:intraday-patterns:3}
Take the other side of a large published imbalance in the auction, sized to the auction's depth, and exit at the next open or during the next day, avoiding
names where the imbalance may carry news.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s1:intraday-patterns:4}
Use round-the-clock futures, not open and close prices; compute returns by hour; account for costs; test stability across periods and markets; and compare
the size of the effect with the sampling error of the split.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s1:intraday-patterns:5}
Gap risk at the open, with no chance to trade out; news concentrated overnight; the cost of two trades a day; and, if the premium is compensation for
overnight risk, its losses come in bad nights.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{iq:s1:intraday-patterns:6}
The delta of a book with gamma $\Gamma$ changes by $\Gamma\,\Delta S$ after a move $\Delta S$; a short-gamma dealer ($\Gamma < 0$) must buy $|\Gamma|\Delta S$ to stay
hedged after a rise. Under linear impact the price moves by $\lambda |\Gamma| \Delta S$, in the direction of the original move: a feedback proportional to the
dealers' gamma.
\end{solution}
