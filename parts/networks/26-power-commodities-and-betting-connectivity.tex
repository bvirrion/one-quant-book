\chapter{Power, Commodities and Betting Connectivity}\label{ch:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity}

Europe's cross-border intraday power market matches orders from twenty-five countries through one \omterm{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sob}{shared order book}, and
allocates the interconnectors' capacity with each trade, first come, first served. When capacity across a border is scarce,
the last megawatt goes to whoever reaches the book first. In the chapter's model a participant \qty{10}{\milli\second} from
the book, against a rival at five, obtains the last megawatt in 4.5\% of the contests; at \qty{2}{\milli\second}, in 98.5\%.

Book~3 described intraday power, market coupling, bidding zones, balancing and schedule nominations, and the betting exchanges.
This chapter is about how firms connect to them: the \omterm{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sob}{shared order book} and the local trading systems in front of it, the
grid operators' scheduling systems, the commodity exchanges of chapter~11's map, and a betting exchange's interfaces and
charges. The capacity race is a labelled simulation; the rules are the operators' own.

\section{Intraday power interfaces and the shared order book}

\begin{definition}[Shared order book, implicit continuous allocation]\label{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sob}
A \emph{shared order book}\index{shared order book} is one order book that several market operators feed with their
participants' orders, so that an order entered with one operator can match an order entered with another.
\emph{Implicit continuous allocation}\index{implicit continuous allocation} is the allocation of cross-border transmission
capacity at the moment two orders in different bidding zones match, with the capacity and the energy priced together and the
capacity used by the trade removed at once from what the next trade can use.
\end{definition}

The single intraday coupling (SIDC) runs one \omterm{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sob}{shared order book}, a
capacity management module holding the cross-zonal capacities the grid operators release, and a shipping module
(\cref{dat:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sidc}). A participant does not connect to the shared book directly: it
trades through its market operator's local trading system, which passes orders to the book and shows it the orders of other
operators' participants where capacity allows. Within a price level, orders rank by the time they were registered; across a
border, a match is possible only while capacity remains, and each match consumes it.

\begin{dated}{2026-09}{The single intraday coupling, from ENTSO-E and a market operator}\label{dat:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sidc}
\omterm{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sob}{Shared order book}, capacity management module and shipping module; ``trade is concluded on a first-come-first-served principle
where the highest buy price and the lowest sell price get served first''; implicit allocation (explicit only at the French--German
and Croatian--Slovenian borders); 25 countries coupled since the fourth wave (November 2022); the cross-zonal gate closure is to move
from 60 to 30 minutes before delivery. Nord Pool's regulations (June 2024): price, then registration time; cross-zonal allocation in
the continuous market suspended during the intraday auctions on the same borders.
\end{dated}

A scarce border is a race. Imagine a price difference opening between two zones with room left on the interconnector for one more
order: every participant who sees it sends an order, and the first to reach the shared book gets the capacity. The chapter's model
has five rivals whose latencies to the book are lognormal around 5, 10, 20, 40 and \qty{80}{\milli\second}, with a spread of 0.3, and
varies the firm's own median (all assumptions: where the book is hosted and how fast each local system is are not published).

\omcode{code/firm/powerlink/firm_powerlink.py}{35}{41}{The capacity race: the first arrivals get the scarce capacity.}\label{lst:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:race}

\begin{proposition}[Scarcity makes latency all or nothing]\label{prop:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:scarce}
When $s$ orders' worth of capacity remains and $n$ participants race, a participant obtains capacity if and only if it is among the
first $s$ arrivals; with one slot, its share is the probability that it is the fastest, which falls from one to zero over a range of
latencies set by the fastest rival's spread; more slots move the fall to the $s$-th fastest rival.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Allocation is by arrival order, so a participant wins exactly when fewer than $s$ others arrive before it. With one slot this is the
event that its latency is below every rival's, whose probability decreases in its own latency and is dominated by the fastest rival.
\end{proof}

The value follows. If the price difference the capacity captures averages EUR 12 per megawatt-hour (an assumption), a firm at
\qty{2}{\milli\second} expects EUR 11.82 per megawatt-hour it offers into such contests and a firm at \qty{10}{\milli\second} EUR 0.54:
the whole value of a scarce border goes to the fastest participant, and the second fastest gets almost nothing. With three orders'
capacity left, the same \qty{10}{\milli\second} firm gets 95.4\% of contests. Scarcity, not the market, turns an intraday power
desk into a latency business, and only on the borders and hours where capacity binds.

The gate closure bounds the race in time: cross-zonal trading for a delivery period stops 60 minutes before it (30 after the planned
change), and during the intraday auctions the continuous market's cross-zonal allocation stops on the auctioned borders. A desk's
clock therefore has three kinds of moment: continuous trading with capacity, auction windows without it, and the gate.

\section{Grid-operator scheduling systems}

After trading, the positions become schedules: a balancing responsible party nominates to its grid operator the energy it will
inject, withdraw and exchange, period by period, before the operator's deadlines. These exchanges are not trading messages but
documents, sent over ENTSO-E's Energy Communication Platform, which offers reliable, secure delivery over AMQP, the MADES web service
(IEC 62325-504) or shared folders. Latency here is minutes, not milliseconds; what matters is that every trade has a schedule, that
the documents are acknowledged, and that a failed delivery is noticed before the deadline, the same reconciliation discipline as a
drop copy (chapter~23), on a slower clock.

\section{Commodity-exchange connectivity}

The commodity futures exchanges are exchanges like the others, on chapter~11's map: ICE Futures Europe's matching in Basildon, the
European energy and metals contracts on the same continental campuses as the financial ones. Their connectivity is chapter~9's:
\omterm{def:nw:colocation-products-and-how-they-are-sold:mmr}{cross-connects}, \omterm{def:nw:colocation-products-and-how-they-are-sold:colo}{colocation}, feeds and gateways, with the fairness questions of chapter~23. What differs for a power or gas desk is
the combination: a futures position on an exchange, an intraday position through a local trading system and the shared book, and
schedules to a grid operator, three connections on three clocks for one physical exposure.

\section{Betting-exchange interfaces and their data charges}

A betting exchange sells access to its order books through an application programming interface, and prices it by what the client
asks of it. Betfair's documentation is explicit (\cref{dat:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:betfair}): data requests are
limited by weight, 200 points per request, where each market in a request costs the weight of the projection asked for; transactions
above a threshold per hour are charged; and the exchange recommends its streaming interface over polling.

\begin{definition}[Transaction charge]\label{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:txn}
A \emph{transaction charge}\index{transaction charge} is a venue's fee on the number of order actions a participant sends above a
free threshold per period, counting placements and failed actions, whatever their outcome, to price the load that frequent
re-quoting places on the venue.
\end{definition}

\begin{dated}{2026-09}{A betting exchange's request limits and charges, from its developer documentation}\label{dat:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:betfair}
Betfair Exchange API: a request's weight (the projection's weight times the number of markets) must not exceed 200 points; best offers
weigh 5 a market, all offers with traded volumes 32. \omterm{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:txn}{Transaction charges} may apply above 5\,000 placed or failed transactions an hour;
a placement and its successful cancellation count once. Live application key: a one-off fee of GBP 499. ``Use the Stream API instead
of polling wherever possible.''
\end{dated}

\omcode{code/firm/powerlink/firm_powerlink.py}{53}{67}{Polling arithmetic from the published weights, and the transactions above the hourly threshold.}\label{lst:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:poll}

Polling 1\,000 markets five times a second for best offers takes 40 markets a request, 25 requests a poll, 125 requests a second; for
the full ladder with traded volumes, 6 markets a request and 835 requests a second. Either way the data is on average
\qty{110}{\milli\second} old with a \qty{10}{\milli\second} round trip, half the polling interval plus the trip. A stream sends each
change once, when it happens. And a strategy re-quoting 30 markets four times a minute sends 7\,200 transactions an hour, 2\,200 above
the threshold: on a venue that charges by the action, the cancel-and-replace habits of an exchange market maker are a cost line.

\begin{method}[Connecting a power or betting desk]\label{met:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:plan}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Map each market to its clock: continuous with capacity, auctions, gate closures, schedule deadlines.
\item For intraday power, measure the latency to the shared book through each local trading system you can use, and find the borders
  and hours where capacity binds; that is where latency pays.
\item Send schedules through the grid operators' platform with acknowledgements, and reconcile trades to schedules before every
  deadline.
\item For betting exchanges, stream instead of polling, and count transactions against the charged threshold.
\item Put commodity futures on chapter~9's footing: \omterm{def:nw:colocation-products-and-how-they-are-sold:colo}{colocation}, feeds, gateways.
\end{enumerate}
\end{method}

\section{Problem: The Last Megawatt Across the Border}

\begin{problem}[{Weekend problem --- latency and scarce cross-border capacity}]\label{pb:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:1}
A power desk trades intraday across a border whose capacity is often scarce. It races five rivals for the last capacity, as in the
chapter's model, and values the spread at EUR 12 per megawatt-hour on average.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part I --- The rules.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item How is cross-border capacity allocated in continuous intraday trading?
\item What happens to capacity when two orders match across the border?
\item When does cross-zonal trading stop for a delivery period, and when might that change?
\item What happens during an intraday auction on the border?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part II --- The race.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item What share of one-slot contests does the desk win at 2 and at \qty{10}{\milli\second}?
\item At what latency does it win half?
\item What changes with three orders' capacity left?
\item Why does the fastest rival's spread matter?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part III --- The value.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item What does the desk expect per megawatt-hour offered at 2 and \qty{10}{\milli\second}?
\item Why does the second-fastest participant get almost nothing?
\item On which borders and hours does latency pay?
\item What does the desk not know, and how would it find out?
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Part IV --- The verdict.}
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item State the \emph{named result}: the probability of obtaining scarce cross-border capacity as a function of latency to the \omterm{def:nw:power-commodities-and-betting-connectivity:sob}{shared
  order book}, and its value per megawatt-hour.
\item Which inputs are published and which assumed?
\item Which local trading system should the desk trade through?
\item What would a common delay in the capacity module change?
\item What should the desk measure?
\item How do schedules fit the same plan?
\item Where does this go in chapter~29's plan?
\item In one sentence: when does an intraday power desk need to be fast?
\end{enumerate}
\end{problem}
