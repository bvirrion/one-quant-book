\chapter{Making a Market}\label{ch:iv2:making-a-market}

\begin{ivtrains}
Giving a bid and an ask on anything, at once, and moving them for the right reason: the value, the distribution, who is on the other side, what their trade just told you and what you already hold. In every question a bid is the price at which you buy and an ask the price at which you sell. The stem fixes the width of your quote and what the other side does, so each has one answer you can check. The early questions take a couple of minutes, and the last ones are derivations.
\end{ivtrains}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:1}
I roll three fair dice, and a contract pays their sum in dollars. Quote a bid and an ask exactly 1 apart, in steps of 0.5. A customer who has seen nothing then buys or sells one lot at your prices, choosing the side on a coin toss. What do you quote, and what do you expect to earn? Would a quote of 11 bid, 12 asked earn less on average?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:2}
I draw one card from a shuffled 52-card deck and look at it. A contract pays its rank, from ace at 1 to king at 13. Quote a bid and an ask exactly 2 apart, in steps of 0.5. I sell you one lot at your bid if the rank is below it, buy one lot at your ask if the rank is above it, and otherwise pass. Which quote loses the least on average, and how much does it lose? If you may choose any whole-number width, what is the narrowest that loses less than 1?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:3}
A fair die is rolled, and a contract pays the square of the result. Quote a bid and an ask exactly 2 apart, in steps of 0.5. A customer who has not seen the die buys or sells one lot, choosing the side on a coin toss. What do you quote?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:4}
In a 52-card deck, count an ace as 1, number cards at face value, a jack as 11, a queen as 12 and a king as 13. Ten cards have been turned face up, and they total 100. One contract pays the value of the next card, and another pays the sum of all 42 cards still face down. You quote each one 1 wide, in steps of 0.25. Your counterparty sees the same ten cards. They buy at your ask if it is below the contract's expected value, sell at your bid if it is above, and otherwise pass. What are your two quotes? Which contract carries more risk for you?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:5}
A die will be rolled. Contract $A$ pays 1 dollar if the result is 4, 5 or 6, and contract $B$ pays 1 dollar if it is 1, 2 or 3. You show $A$ at 0.55 bid, 0.60 asked and $B$ at 0.50 bid, 0.52 asked, and your counterparty takes any combination of one-lot trades that locks in a sure profit. What do they do, and how much do they make? What must your four prices satisfy so that no such combination exists?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:6}
Two fair dice are rolled one after the other, and a contract pays the higher of the two. Quote a bid and an ask exactly 1 apart, in steps of 0.25, before either die is rolled. A customer who has seen nothing buys or sells one lot on a coin toss. Then the first die shows a 3, and you quote again on the same terms. What are your two quotes? What would your second quote have been if the first die had shown a 6?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:7}
Make a market on how many prime numbers there are below 1\,000, with no calculator. A contract pays that number in dollars. Your bid and ask must be exactly 20 apart, in whole numbers. I know the answer: I sell you one lot at your bid if the true number is below it, buy one lot at your ask if it is above, and otherwise pass. What do you quote, and how did you get it?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:8}
You roll a fair die until the first six appears, and a contract pays the number of rolls in dollars. Quote a bid and an ask exactly 2 apart, in whole numbers. A customer who knows nothing buys or sells one lot on a coin toss. What do you quote? Compare the two ways the trade can go: on each, what do you expect to earn, how likely are you to lose money, and how much can you lose?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:9}
A contract pays the number of red cards among the top 10 of a shuffled 52-card deck. Quote a bid and an ask exactly 2 apart, in steps of 0.5. A customer who has not seen the cards buys or sells one lot on a coin toss. What do you quote, and how likely is the final number to land outside your quote? The first card is then turned over and is red. Where do you quote now, on the same terms?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:10}
You believe an unknown quantity $X$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. You quote a bid of $\mu - h$ and an ask of $\mu + h$, and a contract settles at $X$. Your counterparty knows $X$: they sell one lot at your bid if $X$ is below it, buy one lot at your ask if $X$ is above it, and otherwise pass. Find your expected loss as a function of $h$. How much do you lose if your quote is your 90\% confidence interval? What width keeps the loss under 5\% of $\sigma$?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:11}
A contract pays, in dollars, the number of piano tuners in a city of five million. Your rough reasoning gives a best guess of 200 and leaves you unsure by about a factor of 2. Take that to mean the natural logarithm of the number is normal, with its mean at $\ln 200$ and standard deviation $\ln 2$. You may quote any prices, and a customer who knows nothing buys or sells one lot on a coin toss. Around which number do you centre your quote, and why not 200? How likely is the true number to exceed your centre? What range holds the true number with 90\% probability?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:12}
A contract is worth either 80 or 120, equally likely. You quote one bid and one ask, at any prices, and one trader arrives and trades one lot. With probability 30\% the trader knows the true value, and buys if it is 120 and sells if it is 80. Otherwise they know nothing, and buy or sell on a coin toss. At what bid and ask do you exactly break even on average, whichever side they take?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:13}
A contract is worth either 0 or 100, equally likely. Traders arrive one at a time, and each is a new person. Each trades one lot: with probability 20\% they know the value, and buy if it is 100 and sell if it is 0. Otherwise they buy or sell on a coin toss. Before each trader you quote the bid and the ask at which you break even on average, at any prices. The first four trades are buy, buy, sell, buy. What is your quote before each of the first five traders? If the same four trades had come in another order, would your final quote change?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:14}
A fair die has been rolled out of your sight, and a contract pays its value. You quote 3 bid, 4 asked. Your counterparty has looked at the die with probability one half. If they have, they sell one lot at your bid if the value is strictly below it, buy one lot at your ask if it is strictly above, and otherwise pass. If they have not, they buy or sell on a coin toss. You do not know which it is. They sell to you. What is the contract worth now? Where do you quote next, 1 wide and centred on your new value, in steps of 0.1? What is it worth if they had passed instead?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:15}
A contract is worth 90 or 110, equally likely. One trader arrives. With probability 10\% they know the value, and buy if it is 110 and sell if it is 90. Otherwise they buy or sell on a coin toss. A trader who knows the value trades 100 lots with probability 80\% and 1 lot otherwise. A trader who does not trades 100 lots with probability 5\% and 1 lot otherwise. You see the size and side, and you may name a different price for each size. At what ask do you break even on average for a 1-lot buyer, and for a 100-lot buyer? If you must show one ask for both sizes, what is it?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:16}
A contract's fair value is 100 and will not change before it settles at 100. You are long 40 lots, and your score at the end is your profit minus $0.01$ times your final position squared. A customer may buy one lot at your ask or sell one lot at your bid, and may also do neither. What is the lowest ask and the highest bid at which a trade would not lower your score? Your quote must be exactly 2 wide, centred between those two prices: what do you show? What do you show if you are short 40? How far does your centre move after each trade?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader} \iqfirm{market maker, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:17}
A contract's fair value is fixed at 100, and you always quote 99 bid, 101 asked. Each round, exactly one customer arrives and buys or sells one lot on a coin toss. Your position must stay between $-3$ and $+3$ lots: at $+3$ you take your bid down, and at $-3$ you take your ask down, so a customer on that side goes away with no trade. You start flat, and any final position is marked at 100. What is your expected profit over 10 rounds? What is it per round in the long run? What is it per round in the long run with a limit of $\pm L$ lots?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QD} \iqfirm{market maker, bank, crypto} \iqlevel{junior}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:18}
A contract $S$ pays the sum of three fair dice. Another dealer quotes a contract on each single die at 3.4 bid, 3.6 asked, in any size, and will keep doing so. You must show a quote on $S$. Your counterparty takes any trades that lock in a sure profit against you and that dealer. For which bids and which asks can they do so? A separate customer, who knows nothing, then buys one lot of $S$ from you at your ask of 10.7. What do you make if you hedge at once with the single dice, and what if you do not hedge?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:19}
Three fair dice $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ are rolled out of your sight. Contract $A$ pays $X + Y$ and contract $B$ pays $X + Z$, and you quote each one at 6.5 bid, 7.5 asked. Your counterparty knows the sum $X + Y$ and nothing else. They buy one lot of $A$ at your ask if that sum is above it, sell at your bid if it is below, and otherwise pass. They buy $A$ from you. What is each contract worth now? Given what you now know, how many lots of $B$, fractions allowed, should you buy to make the variance of your position as small as possible, and how much of it does that remove?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QD, LLE} \iqfirm{market maker, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:20}
You quote a contract at its fair value plus and minus $h$. Between one piece of news and the next, 10 uninformed customers trade one lot each with you, and each earns you $h$. The next piece of news then moves the fair value by 2, up or down. With probability 80\% a fast trader hits your old quote on the side that is now wrong, for one lot, before you can move it. Otherwise you move in time. What $h$ makes you break even? What is it if a faster system of yours cuts that 80\% to 50\%, and what if there are only 5 customers?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:21}
A contract is worth 98 or 102, equally likely. Each trader trades one lot: 10\% of them know the value and buy if it is 102 and sell if it is 98, and the rest buy or sell on a coin toss. A rival shows 99 bid, 101 asked. You may join at the same prices, in which case every trade is split equally between you. Or you may show $100 - h$ bid, $100 + h$ asked for some $h$ below 1, in steps of 0.1, and take every trade. What do you earn per trader in each case, and which $h$ is best if the rival stays put? Where do quotes end up if the rival also moves, one step at a time?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:22}
A contract pays the sum of three fair dice, and you quote $10.5 - h$ bid, $10.5 + h$ asked. With probability one half your counterparty has looked at one die. They then work out the expected sum, sell one lot if it is below your bid, buy one lot if it is above your ask, and otherwise pass. Otherwise they have seen nothing and buy or sell on a coin toss. What $h$ makes you break even on average? What is it if they look with probability one quarter, or three quarters?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:23}
A contract is worth 0 or 100, equally likely. Traders arrive one at a time, and each is a new person. Half of them know the value, and buy if it is 100 and sell if it is 0. The other half buy or sell on a coin toss. Before each trader you quote the bid and the ask at which you break even on average, at any prices. Every trader so far has sold. What is your quote before the first five traders? When does your width first fall below 5? How does the width behave as the sales go on, and why? How likely is a run of four sales if the value is really 100?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:24}
A contract's value $V$ is uniform on 0 to 100. One trader arrives and trades one lot. With probability $\alpha$ they know $V$: they buy at your ask if $V$ is above it, sell at your bid if $V$ is below it, and otherwise pass. With probability $1 - \alpha$ they know nothing, and buy or sell on a coin toss. Find the ask at which a purchase makes you break even on average, and the bid at which a sale does, as functions of $\alpha$. Evaluate them at $\alpha = 0.5$. What happens as $\alpha$ approaches 1?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:25}
A contract's value $V$ is uniform on 0 to 100, and you quote $50 - h$ bid, $50 + h$ asked. One trader arrives. With probability 20\% they know $V$: they buy one lot at your ask if $V$ is above it, sell one lot at your bid if $V$ is below it, and otherwise pass. With probability 80\% they know nothing: they pick a side on a coin toss and trade one lot with probability $1 - h/20$, and not at all if $h$ is 20 or more. Which $h$ maximises your expected profit, and what is that profit? At which smaller $h$ do you only break even? Above what share of knowing traders is no quote profitable?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:26}
A contract's fair value is 100 and stays there until it settles. Your score is your profit minus $0.01$ times your final position squared. Exactly one customer comes, wanting to buy or sell one lot on a coin toss. A seller takes your bid with probability $e^{-2\delta}$, where $\delta$ is how far your bid sits below 100. A buyer takes your ask with probability $e^{-2\delta}$, with $\delta$ measured above 100. A bid above 100 or an ask below 100 is taken for certain. Which bid and ask maximise your expected score when you are flat, and when you are long 40? From what position does your ask reach 100?
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:27}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{junior, experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:28}

Each of you offers to sell one lot at your own die plus 3.5 plus a markup $m$, the same $m$ for both, and you both know this rule. A customer always buys one lot from the lower ask, and if the asks are equal you each sell half a lot. What is your expected profit at $m = 0.5$? What $m$ makes you break even? \begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:30}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:31}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QT, Trader, QR} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund, crypto} \iqlevel{experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:33}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QR, QT} \iqfirm{market maker, hedge fund} \iqlevel{experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:34}
A stock's true value $V$ is normal with mean 100 and standard deviation 2. Uninformed customers send a net order $u$, normal with mean 0 and standard deviation 100 shares, independent of $V$. One insider knows $V$ and, without seeing $u$, sends an order of $x$ shares to maximise expected profit. You see only the total $y = x + u$, and you fill it all at a price that makes you break even given $y$. Assume the insider's order is linear in $V$ and your price is $100 + \lambda y$. Find $\lambda$, the insider's order and expected profit, and how much of the variance of $V$ remains after the price is set.
\end{question}

\begin{question}[$\star\star\star\star$ \iqroles{QR, QT} \iqfirm{market maker, crypto} \iqlevel{experienced}]\label{iq:iv2:making-a-market:35}
A stock trades at a mid of 100. You hold 2 shares, and at the end of the period the mid will be normal with mean 100 and variance 4. You have exponential utility of final wealth, with risk aversion 0.1, so the utility of wealth $w$ is $-e^{-0.1 w}$. You post one bid and one ask. Exactly one customer comes, wanting to buy or to sell one share on a coin toss. A seller takes a bid $\delta$ below 100, and a buyer takes an ask $\delta$ above 100, with probability $e^{-1.5\delta}$, for $\delta \ge 0$. Derive the bid and ask that maximise your expected utility. What are they? How do they split into a shift caused by your position and a width? What happens as the risk aversion goes to 0?
\end{question}
