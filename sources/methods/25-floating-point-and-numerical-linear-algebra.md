# 25. Floating Point and Numerical Linear Algebra — brief and source ledger

## Brief

- **Hook.** In January 1982 the Vancouver Stock Exchange launched an index at 1000.000; twenty-two months later it stood near 520 in a rising market. It had been truncated, not rounded, to three decimals at each of thousands of daily recalculations; recomputed, it was about 1098.
- **Sections.** Representation; Cancellation and the accumulation of error; Conditioning and stability; Factorisations; Iterative solvers; The bugs that cost money.
- **Defines.** floating-point number, machine epsilon, unit in the last place, subnormal number, fused multiply-add, catastrophic cancellation, compensated summation, Welford's algorithm, condition number, backward error, backward stability, LU factorisation, Cholesky factorisation, QR factorisation, singular value decomposition, tridiagonal matrix algorithm, conjugate gradient method, preconditioner, bitwise reproducibility.
- **Uses (defined earlier).** index divisor, index point, variance, covariance matrix, ordinary least squares, ridge regression, multicollinearity.
- **Results (named theorems, not terms).** relative rounding error at most the unit roundoff (standard model); error bound of recursive and compensated summation; the textbook one-pass variance is unstable; Welford's update is stable; forward error is at most condition number times backward error; Cholesky succeeds iff the matrix is positive definite; normal equations square the condition number; CG converges in at most n steps; rate governed by sqrt(cond).
- **Tutorial.** Reproduce the Vancouver drift by simulation; sum a million P&L entries naively, pairwise and with Kahan's compensation; compute a variance three ways on prices near 10,000; watch a Cholesky factorisation fail on a pairwise-estimated correlation matrix; compare C++ and Rust bit for bit with and without FMA contraction.
- **Build.** `firm.fpkit`: numerics kit (Neumaier and pairwise summation, Welford mean, variance and covariance, log-sum-exp, a Thomas tridiagonal solver, Cholesky with a diagnostic and optional jitter, conjugate gradient); Python, C++20 and Rust, with a bit-identity test across the three.
- **Weekend problem.** The index that lost half its value — named result: the expected drift from truncation per recalculation times the number of recalculations, against the documented shortfall of the Vancouver index.
- **Facts to verify.** Vancouver Stock Exchange index truncation error 1982-1983: start 1000.000 (January 1982), level near 524.811 before correction, corrected about 1098.892 (November 1983), from a primary or reputable press source; IEEE Std 754-1985 / 754-2008 / 754-2019 binary64 format and rounding; GCC default -ffp-contract=fast outside ISO C mode (GCC manual); JPMorgan Chase Management Task Force report, 16 January 2013: VaR spreadsheet divided by a sum instead of an average (Chief Investment Office losses); Kahan 1965 (Communications of the ACM); Welford 1962 (Technometrics); Hestenes and Stiefel 1952 (J. Research NBS); Higham, Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms (2002); Goldberg 1991 (ACM Computing Surveys).

## EXCLUDED

- The Vancouver recalculation rate: the source says "approximately 3000" a day; the chapter's simulation uses 2,400, the rate implied by the source's own "around 25 points per month" (0.0005 x 2,400 x ~21 days = 25) and by the documented shortfall of 574 points over 480 trading days; both figures are given in the text. The claim that the market "had in fact risen" rests on the corrected value 1098.892 > 1000 (F1).
- The claim in some retellings that the index was "about 520" or "near 500" is not used; only the documented 524.811.

## Brief deviations

- Tutorial compares Python, C++20 and Rust (all three twins) rather than C++ and Rust only; the FMA comparison is made with an exact rational fused multiply-add in Python and std::fma / f64::mul_add in the twins, since the default build flags cannot contract on x86-64.
